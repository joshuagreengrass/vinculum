# Chapter 6 — Confessio

The Hog's Head at ten o'clock on a Saturday morning smells of stale beer and goat.

Josh comes through the tapestry passage, the same one as ten days ago, alone this time. He crosses the village by the back streets with his hood up and goes in without looking at the barman, who doesn't look at him either. There's a staircase at the back, behind a curtain that was once red. Upstairs, a room with a table, two chairs, a window boarded over with planks.

Corvus is already seated. He has a cup in front of him. It isn't tea; at the Hog's Head nothing is tea.

"You're punctual," he says. "You're always punctual. It's one of the things I find most reassuring about you."

Josh sits across from him. He doesn't take off his cloak.

"Four," he says.

"Four."

"I said three."

"You said three." Corvus turns the cup on the table, slowly, a quarter turn. "In your father's armchair, with great confidence. I remember. It struck me as a clean number."

"Who's the fourth?"

Corvus looks at him a moment. Not with anger. With something worse: curiosity.

"I was hoping you'd tell me."

Josh doesn't answer. He's spent the whole week searching and hasn't found it. He went over the dockside cell, the three names, the shipments. He went over what he knows and what he thinks he knows. There's a smooth space where the answer should be, and he can't touch it.

"The silversmith," says Corvus.

Josh feels something that has no name. It isn't surprise. It's what one feels stepping onto a stair one was certain was there.

The silversmith. The man with skin like wax, the safe behind the stained mirror. The one who signed with Mercer, at the same table, the pact that Josh himself bound with his own blood. Twelve shipments in exchange for silence.

Twelve. The twelfth arrived on Thursday at seven.

The silence ended with it.

"The pact was fulfilled the moment Mercer received the last shipment," says Corvus, like someone reading a paragraph from a familiar book. "And a fulfilled pact binds no one. So when the Ministry asked Mercer who he worked with, Mercer no longer had any magical reason to keep the silversmith's name to himself." He takes a sip. "You knew that better than anyone. You bound it."

"I knew it."

"And?"

Josh has no "and." He has the calculation, the one he made at the Manor so carefully, and in the calculation there's a line that isn't there. He doesn't know whether he never wrote it or wrote it and it was erased.

"I didn't count it," he says.

It's the first time in a long while he's said something like that out loud in front of Corvus. The words come out flat, but he hears them land, and he knows Corvus hears them too.

"You didn't count it," Corvus repeats. He sets down the cup. "The silversmith knows your name, Greengrass."

"I know."

"He received you in his back room twice. The second time, three weeks ago, with Miss Ignotus. If the Ministry gets to him before we do, and he decides your name is worth enough to trade for his freedom…"

"It won't get to him."

"No," says Corvus. "It won't."

He says it in the same tone as everything else. Josh understands what he means. He doesn't ask how. He doesn't ask when. He doesn't ask whether the silversmith closed for a few days as he recommended, saying he was ill, or whether someone knocked on his door anyway.

He doesn't ask. It's what he does best.

"The legacy has taken care of your line," says Corvus. "This time. I'm entering it in your account, not mine."

"Understood."

Corvus studies him for a long while. Then he leans a little over the table.

"I've known you for two years, and in two years you've never got a calculation wrong. Not one. It's what makes you so expensive." A pause. "I'm not interested in why this time you did. I'm interested in it not happening again."

"It won't happen again."

"Good."

Corvus stands. Adjusts his cloak. At the door, before going down, he stops.

"You're different," he says, without turning around. "I don't know how. But you are."

He goes down the stairs. Josh is left alone in the room with the boarded window, Corvus's cup cooling on the table.

He looks at his hands. They're still. It takes him a second too long to be sure they're his.

---

That night, the seventh-year dormitory is empty.

The others went down to the party the sixth-years put together in the common room: music, butterbeer someone smuggled in, shouts that come muffled from the other side of the door, through the stone. Josh cast a Silencing Charm on the door and drew the curtains of his bed, and now the party is a distant murmur, like the lake on the other side of the glass.

Atis is lying beside him.

She came along the dungeon corridor with her hood up and her heart in her throat, as she put it, because she'd never been down to Slytherin and was convinced the wall would give her away. It didn't. Now she's on her back, her head on his arm, looking at the dark green canopy as if it were a new sky.

"It's nicer than I thought," she says. "I thought you slept in coffins."

"Coffins are for the fifth-years."

She laughs. Then she goes quiet for a while, and he feels her gathering a question.

"Tell me a goal," she says at last. "Something you want. Small or big, doesn't matter."

Josh thinks about it. Not for long.

"I'm stuck on a spell," he says. "A blood one. If I mastered it, I'd have more… standing. They'd move me up."

"In the neo-order?"

"In the neo-order."

She turns her head to look at him. That he's in there, that he has a place, she had suspected. The way one suspects these things about certain surnames: the ones left over after Voldemort, the ones who never quite went away. Beyond that, Josh knows she knows nothing. Not how they work, not what they do, not what it means for someone to move up.

"So your goal is to rise in there." She says it slowly, testing how it sounds. "What for?"

"Because it's the only thing that helps me keep the people I care about safe. And myself."

She doesn't answer right away. She looks at him with that still attention she gives creatures she doesn't understand yet.

"Sounds like it all falls on you."

"I handle pressure well."

"I believe you." She settles against him. "I mean, you're very young to have a position in there. I always pictured them all old, angry, waiting for him to come back."

Josh looks at the canopy. He thinks of a chair pulled out for him at a long table, at fourteen. Of Corvus's voice that same morning, saying *it's what makes you so expensive*.

"I'm the last Greengrass outside Azkaban who can open the family grimoires," he says. "Since they killed my sister. And the neo-order is interested in exactly that. What can be done without a wand."

She props herself up a little on one elbow.

"Blood magic?" He nods. "I know it exists. I read something. But nobody explains it, you know? It's one of those subjects that gets put in a drawer nobody opens."

"There's a good reason for that."

"What?"

"For a start, it takes a pact with a demon."

She blinks.

"You have a pact with a demon?"

"The family does. The whole line. For centuries."

"Okay." She sits all the way up, legs crossed on the quilt, her hair a mess. "Okay. Sorry, but now I have way too many questions and I don't know which to start with. I don't know how any of it works. How does the pact work? How does the blood thing work? Is your blood a different color? Are you like a unicorn?"

Josh laughs. The second time in a week. He still isn't used to the sound.

He kisses her forehead and sits up too, leaning against the headboard.

---

"Centuries ago," he says, "an ancestor of mine made a pact with a demon. I can't say its name. That's part of the pact: it isn't spoken, it isn't written."

She nods, suddenly serious.

"All magic has a cost," he goes on. "Always. That's what wands are for: the core channels part of the energy so the body doesn't pay all of it. That's why very few wizards do wandless magic. That's what they teach here."

"Yes. Up to there, yes."

"With blood magic there's nothing in between." He pauses, choosing how to go on. He's never had to explain this to anyone outside the neo-order. "How easily shocked are you?"

She raises an eyebrow.

"If you think I'm going to faint, no. Do you think I'm going to get scared?"

"I think it's a reasonable question."

He reaches over to the nightstand. Opens the drawer. Takes out the dagger.

It's small, black, its blade barely longer than his hand. In the wandlight, the runes engraved along the edge seem to move, though they don't.

"With a wand, you ask," he says. "And the world obeys. With this you don't ask. You pay. It all starts with a cut. The wrist, the palm. The tongue, if it's a sealing. And then the blood does what I want, not what blood would do. It stretches, folds, draws in the air without falling. But only if I ask it to. Never on its own, never by accident."

She looks at the dagger. She doesn't touch it.

"The rules are few," he goes on. "Without real blood, nothing works. It doesn't bring back life. I can't heal myself with my own. And what the body loses in each ritual doesn't come back. There's no cure that I know of."

"And those runes?"

"Ancient magic. The pact is made on this dagger. That's why I always keep it close."

She leans in to look at them more closely. She frowns; they aren't runes taught in any class.

"And if you lost it?"

"I can't." He turns the dagger in his hand to show her the other side. "It finds me. There were ancestors who tried to get rid of it. Throw it away, bury it, leave it in another country. It always turned up again at the manor. At first they thought it was some elf's joke."

He says it with half a smile, and in the same movement rests his thumb where he shouldn't.

It doesn't hurt. It almost never hurts, with this blade. He feels only a thin cold at the pad of his thumb, and when he looks, there's a drop.

Dark. Almost black outside the body. Dense. Gleaming in the wandlight. It doesn't fall. It stays there, round, still, on the tip of his finger, waiting for someone to tell it what to do.

---

He was fifteen and

it smelled of melted wax. Of iron. Of something sweet beneath the iron.

A circle on the floor. Not chalk: dried blood, his, from before. Candles. Many. Someone behind him with their breathing

he doesn't remember who.

The dagger in his hand. His arm outstretched. The cut, long, from the wrist

and then the cold. Not in his eyes this time. In his whole arm, climbing, like ice water entering through the wound and creeping up inside, and the arm that wouldn't move, that went stiff as wood, and the black veins climbing past the elbow, fast, too fast

something that spoke.

Not with a voice. With the voice of his own blood, if blood had a voice. It said his name. It said another name he couldn't repeat and that no longer

there was someone in a chair.

In front of him. Bound. A face. The face

the face was another face. One second. A beloved face over a stranger's face, like two drawings on the same sheet of tracing paper, and the beloved face had the shadowed eyes of the bad nights and

no.

Something hot ran down his cheeks. It wasn't tears. It tasted of iron.

The person in the chair was screaming. Or he was screaming. Or no one.

Then

Then nothing. A smooth space. Minutes that aren't there.

Then the cold floor against his face, and the candle out, and his arm burning as if it had been thrust into fire, and the empty chair. And a knowledge he didn't remember learning, lodged behind his eyes, like a stone.

He never knew how long he lay on the floor.

He never knew who had been in the chair.

---

"Josh. You cut yourself."

Atis is holding his wrist. Carefully, as if it were something that could break. She looks at the dark drop on the tip of his thumb with wide eyes, and then she looks at him, and he sees in her face that seconds have passed. More than he thinks.

"It's nothing," he says.

He wipes his finger on the sheet. A dark stain, the size of a fingertip. One more.

"You went away again," she says slowly. "Like in the corridor. Like with the thestrals."

It isn't a question. He doesn't answer it as if it were. He puts the dagger back in the drawer, blade facing inward. Closes the drawer. Keeps his hand on the wood a moment.

"What I lose," he says, "in each ritual, isn't blood. Blood is the cheap part. You lose it and it comes back."

She waits.

"The expensive part is something else. Each big ritual takes a piece of… continuity. I don't know what to call it better. It doesn't hurt. It's stranger than that. Sometimes I look at my hands and don't recognize them. Or I see a pact I signed and feel that the one who signed it wasn't me." A pause. "Sometimes I lose a few seconds. Like just now."

She says nothing for a while. She runs her thumb over his wrist, absently, over the place where the sleeve, for the first time, isn't buttoned.

"Is it like losing your memory?" she asks at last. "Or is it like… compartmentalizing?"

"If I had to give it a name, I'd say wear. What ties me to the one who started all this keeps thinning. Ritual by ritual."

"And in the end it kills you?"

"I don't know." He says it honestly, and realizes it's the first time he's said it out loud. "There's no one who's reached the bottom and left it in writing. There's no line in the grimoires that says 'this far.' But in the diaries of the ones who wrote the grimoires, you can tell. Page by page. By the end they don't write the way they did at the start." He shrugs. "The cost is identity, I suppose."

"And you can't stop doing them?"

He looks at the canopy.

"No."

She notices it. The change in his voice. She notices and doesn't push, and after a moment she says, quietly:

"Because you have to keep the people you care about safe."

"That too."

Josh bites his tongue for a second. Then he decides. He feels it the way one feels a cut: a fine line, clean, and on the other side something that can't be put back.

"The pact is in the blood of the whole family. If you don't use the magic, the curse shows up. That's what happened to Tori. My sister. She couldn't use it without it hurting her, and if she didn't use it, the curse ate her anyway. She got sicker. Slowly. Until she had almost no magic left."

She doesn't move.

"And then the people I work with decided she was worth more as bait. They sent her on a mission. They left her behind. And she was killed."

"Bait?" she repeats.

"She was no use to them anymore." He says it with a bitterness he doesn't bother to hide. "That's how they work."

Atis takes a deep breath. He hears her do it.

"I don't understand," she admits. "I mean, I understand what you're telling me. I don't understand how someone… how something like that gets decided."

"I'm going to tell you what it suits me for you to know," he says. "The rest isn't for you. And I'm asking you not to take the limit as a discourtesy."

She nods. He begins.

It comes out in a voice that isn't entirely his. A learned voice, worn from hearing it so often in rooms with fireplaces and long tables. The previous project didn't fall through bad luck but through falling short. Persecuting Muggle-borns and half-bloods was always insufficient, badly aimed. The new doctrine is stricter: every magical family outside the Sacred Twenty-Eight is surplus. No exceptions. And they don't consider it prejudice but fact. Blood magic, Parseltongue, ancient magic, the erasing of traces: none of it comes from a wand. It comes from the blood. To dilute it is…

"It's okay," she says softly. "You don't have to go on if you don't want to."

He stops. Lowers his eyes.

She keeps looking at the closed drawer. He watches her face change as she understands: not the content, but the circle. Where she is, within that circle. Or outside it.

"And the other families?" she asks, cautiously. "The ones that aren't among the twenty-eight. What do they do with them?"

He nods, without looking at her. He's ashamed. He didn't know he could still be ashamed.

"The pawns. People they recruit without believing in them. Desperate enough to want to belong. Expendable enough to send into the dangerous work."

"And what happens to them?"

Josh thinks of a field in the north. Of a man with a single boot.

"Whatever's necessary to save the ones inside," he says.

---

She settles against the headboard, next to him, shoulder to shoulder, looking straight ahead. For a long while, neither of them says anything. On the other side of the door, the party rises and falls like a tide.

"Well," she says at last. "That was a lot."

"I know."

"I figured there was something behind it. But I didn't think it was so…" She searches for the word and doesn't find it. "Being in there must be exhausting."

"It isn't exhausting. It's convenient." He says it looking at the canopy. "And I'm not asking you to understand the difference. I'm asking you not to credit me with a conviction I don't have. I don't believe in the legacy. I don't believe in purity, or in the twenty-eight, or in any of what they repeat in ceremonial tones. It's a structure. It has access, contacts, resources. I know how to use them. For me it's a means. Never an end."

A pause.

"That doesn't make me clean. Don't think I'm absolving myself. I'm inside. I work for them. What I do costs real people dearly." He thinks of a silversmith who closed for a few days saying he was ill, and pushes the thought away. "But I have rules. Few. I don't kill for ideology. I don't use this against children or against anyone who can't defend themselves. And a pact sealed in blood isn't broken. It would be breaking my own word. And that, literally, costs me."

She turns her head and looks at him.

"So it isn't your ideology."

"No."

"Because if it were, it'd be pretty strange for me to be here." A crooked smile escapes her. "Being one of the surplus."

He doesn't answer. There's nothing to say to that which wouldn't sound like an apology.

"Thanks for telling me," she says. "You didn't have to. And I won't repeat it. To anyone. It's yours."

"Don't thank me." He runs his thumb over his palm again, over the oldest line, without noticing he's doing it. "I like that you know me. You should know who you're with. My life is this, and it's going to go on being this."

She looks at the gesture. The thumb on the palm. She looks at it as if counting how many times he's done it before.

"The only thing I believe," he goes on, "is that blood, name and loyalty don't save anyone. They didn't save Tori. The only thing that protects you is being useful. That's why I stay there. I'll stay as long as it suits me."

His voice drops.

"And mine… what little is left… is the only thing I don't measure by that yardstick. If I ever had to choose between them and anyone else, I wouldn't think twice. I wouldn't fail again."

And then, lower still, almost without meaning to:

"It amazes me how fast you got onto that list. And it scares me."

She goes still.

"Well," she says, after a moment, as if she hadn't quite heard the last part. "I'm glad to know. At least I'm not in the dark anymore, like a little while ago. All I knew about any of this was one library book that said nothing. Now I understand why it said nothing."

He turns his head.

"I understand my mum a bit more now, too," she goes on. "And why my dad always wanted to keep me away from…" She makes a vague gesture with her hand, taking in the canopy, the dungeons, the dagger in the drawer. "From all this."

Her voice fades as she names him. *My dad.* Josh hears it, and hears what she doesn't say, too, and doesn't ask.

And then she turns abruptly, her cheeks burning.

"Wait. I'm on the list?"

"You went to the library to read about my magic?"

He asks it before answering. He doesn't know why. He knows he's looking her in the eyes for the first time in a long while.

"Yes," she says.

"Yes," he says.

---

He takes her face in both hands and kisses her.

She smiles against his mouth.

"I also looked up things about the forest at Durmstrang," she confesses, between one kiss and the next.

"The forest? What for?"

"Because the voices started there." She says it the way one mentions the weather. "That forest is alive. Not like the one here. It's something else. I wanted to know if there was anything written about what happens to me. Anything."

Josh draws back slightly to look at her.

"A forest isn't a demon," he says. "But it's still a pact. Something that gives nothing for free and collects one way or another. Don't take it lightly."

"I don't take it lightly." She runs her fingers over his cheek. "And now I'm more relieved that mine is a forest and not a demon."

"Now."

"Now." She shrugs and steals another kiss. "I wanted to be important to you."

"You're managing it."

"I don't know how. Maybe you're the one using Amortentia."

"I must be," he says, "if after everything I've told you you're still here."

---

She falls asleep first.

Josh stays awake, looking at the canopy, his arm gone numb beneath her head and the party fading little by little on the other side of the door. On the sheet there's a dark stain, the size of a fingertip. Tomorrow he has to clean it. He's going to clean it.

He told her about the demon. The dagger. Tori. The pawns. The doctrine, in his own borrowed voice. He told her what each ritual costs him, something he'd never told anyone, not even Arabella, who has watched him pay that cost more times than anyone.

He didn't tell her about the Ministry. He didn't tell her about Thursdays, about the short-haired young man, about the silver goblets that were seven and not eight.

He didn't tell her that this morning he was told a man who knew his name won't get anywhere, and that he didn't ask how.

He didn't tell her about the circle of candles, the chair, the minutes that aren't there. That he couldn't tell her. You can't confess what you don't remember.

Atis breathes slowly against his shoulder. One of her hands has come to rest on his chest. At the foot of the bed, in the inner pocket of the robes hanging there, lies a Galleon that hasn't entirely cooled.

Josh does the calculation, one more time.

He told her everything that was true.

He didn't tell her everything he is.
