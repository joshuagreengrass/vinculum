# Chapter 2 — Solutio

Hogsmeade smells of burnt sugar and wet leaves.

On the first Saturday of October the village fills with black robes and four-colored scarves moving in flocks along the high street: from Honeydukes to Zonko's, from Zonko's to the Three Broomsticks, from the Three Broomsticks to any shop window with something to look at. The third-years run. The seventh-years walk as if they no longer cared, and they do. The sky is low and white, and the wind coming down off the mountains carries the year's first real cold.

Josh walks with a group of Slytherins to the door of the Three Broomsticks. He lets them go in. He stays a step behind, adjusting his scarf, and when the door closes behind the last of them, he is no longer there.

He doesn't hurry. No one who hurries goes unnoticed. He goes up the side street that leads to the post office, turns before reaching it, follows the dirt path along the last houses to where the village becomes a stone wall and the wall becomes field. Behind a hedge, where no one looks because there's nothing to look at, he stops.

He has until five. At five the prefects start gathering stragglers on the high street. At half past five, the last group passes through the castle gates. By then he has to be walking among them, a Honeydukes bag in hand and the bored air of someone who spent the afternoon eating Chocolate Frogs.

He closes his eyes. Thinks of the destination with his usual precision.

Turns.

---

Knockturn has no sky. Or it has one, but no one looks at it.

The alley closes in on itself like a throat: façades leaning toward one another, grimy shop windows, signs promising things best not read twice. It's earlier here than in Hogsmeade, or it seems later; the light never quite makes up its mind. It smells of damp, of smoke from something that isn't firewood, of old metal.

Arabella is where she said she'd be.

Leaning against the wall across from a secondhand silver shop, hands in the pockets of a coat too big for her, looking at the window as if weighing whether it's worth robbing. She doesn't look up when Josh appears at the end of the alley. She doesn't look up as he approaches. Only when he stops a step away does she look at him.

The patches are still. He always notices that first: the pale areas on her skin, asymmetrical, running down the side of her neck until they disappear into the collar of her coat, spattering the back of one hand and not the other, splitting her left cheek like a map of a coastline that doesn't exist. Still means she hasn't worked today. Or that she worked a long time ago.

The scar on her lip is the second thing. A thin line cutting her upper lip almost at the center, which tightens slightly when she decides something amuses her.

Now it tightens.

"You're late."

"I'm here at the time I said."

"You said one."

"It's one."

She looks at the sky no one looks at, as if she could read the time there.

"It's two past one, Greengrass."

"Then the Hogsmeade clock runs slow."

"All your clocks run slow." She pushes off the wall. "Inside?"

"Inside."

---

The supplier is a thin man with skin the color of candle wax and the eyes of someone who has slept very little in the last three weeks. He receives them in the back room, behind a worn velvet curtain, among shelves of mismatched spoons, candelabra without their pairs, and trays that someone, once, used to serve things in houses that no longer exist.

"Mr. Greengrass." He says it low, as if the name could be heard from the street. "I didn't know if you'd come."

"I told you I'd come."

"Yes. Yes, of course. It's just that after what happened on Thursday…"

"What happened on Thursday is no concern of yours," says Josh, courteously, without changing his tone. "What concerns you is in your safe."

The man looks at Arabella. Arabella doesn't look at him; she's going over the back room with her eyes, corner by corner, like someone taking measurements. The man swallows and turns toward the back wall.

The safe is behind a stained mirror. The man opens it with his wand, his hand trembling enough to need two tries. Inside, among envelopes and bags of Galleons, is a parchment folded in three and tied with a black cord.

Josh receives it with both hands.

He recognizes it before untying it. A year ago, in this same back room, he stood on the other side of this paper: Mercer at one end of the table, the supplier at the other, both with a fresh cut on their thumb and the faces of people who don't yet fully understand what they're signing. Twelve shipments. Silence in exchange. The pact sealed with the blood of both parties and with that of Josh, who bound it.

The twelfth shipment arrived on Thursday at seven.

He unties the cord. Spreads the parchment on the table. At the foot, three rust-colored stains, dry, the size of a fingertip. The two from the signatories, a little crooked. His, perfect, in the center.

"The pact is fulfilled," says Josh. "It no longer binds you to anything. What remains is the imprint, and the imprint is what cannot remain."

"And if I burn it?" asks the supplier. "I could have burned it. I thought about it."

"If you burned it, the imprint would stay in the ash, in the safe, and in you." Josh unbuttons his left cuff. "You did well not to think too hard."

The man backs up to the curtain. Arabella stays where she is, on the other side of the table, hands still in her pockets.

Josh pushes up his sleeve. His forearm is bared, pale, crossed with fine lines, some white, some still pink. He feels Arabella's gaze pass over them and move on, without stopping, the way one passes something already seen many times.

He takes a short-bladed penknife from his robes. He rests it on his left palm, on the oldest line of all, and cuts. A little. Just enough.

The blood comes out red. Outside, it darkens. It grows dense, almost black, almost oil, and stays on his palm without falling, waiting.

Josh leans over the parchment. To undo a pact you must first open it, and to open it you must name it with the same word it was closed with. He lays his hand over the three stains and says it, low, clear:

"*Nexum.*"

---

He was sixteen and he had been told not to ask questions.

The back room was a different one, deeper in the alley, with no window. A table, two chairs, a lamp. Corvus seated in one of the chairs as if the chair were his. He standing, because no one had told him to sit. The hawthorn wand on the table, between them, like an object that no longer entirely belonged to him.

The door opened without anyone knocking.

It was a girl. Younger than him, by a year, perhaps. Thin, in a coat too big for her, her face split by pale patches that at first he took for bad lighting. She didn't say hello. She looked at the wand on the table before looking at either of them.

"That's it," said Corvus, without getting up. "Come in."

She came in. She didn't sit either.

"Greengrass, this is Miss Ignotus." Corvus made a vague gesture with his hand, the same he would have used to indicate a piece of furniture. "She works in the alley. She erases things."

"What things?"

"Whatever she's paid to erase." Corvus allowed himself half a smile. "Traces. Imprints. What's left in a wand after a spell that shouldn't have been cast. She leaves none of her own, you know. Ever. They say the Ministry kept her in a file for years without knowing what to do about it."

"They say a lot of things," she said.

It was the first thing he heard her say. Her voice was flat, with no accent he could place. She had a scar on her lip.

"They do," Corvus conceded. "The Auror Department has a record of what that wand did last month. They haven't read it yet. When they do, I don't want them to find anything."

"What would they find?"

"That isn't part of the job."

She shrugged. Neither curiosity nor indifference: the exact measure of someone who has already decided the answers don't change the price.

"Before you touch anything," said Corvus, "Mr. Greengrass is going to seal an agreement with you. What you see here, what you erase, what you know about him: it doesn't leave this room. Ever."

She looked at Josh for the first time, really looked. Long. Unhurried.

"With blood?"

"With blood."

"I charge double for pacts."

"We know."

Josh cut his palm. She cut her thumb without blinking, with a blade that came out of somewhere in her coat, and when her blood touched his on the table nothing happened that could be seen, but he felt the knot close, the binding tighten like a rope between two points. He said the word. She heard it. He saw that she heard it: saw that she kept it.

Then she laid two fingers on the hawthorn wand.

The patches moved.

It wasn't subtle. The pale areas of her face, her neck, her hands began to shift beneath her skin like ink in water, like clouds across a sped-up sky, shapes forming and dissolving without ever stopping. He couldn't stop looking at them. She didn't seem to notice, or didn't care.

It took a minute. Perhaps less. When she lifted her fingers she was pale, and the patches were still moving.

"Done," she said. "Now it's a wand that never did anything interesting."

Corvus put a bag on the table. She counted it without opening it, by weight. Pocketed it. At the door she stopped.

"Next time, Greengrass," she said, without turning around, "make it something you don't have to hide from your own side."

And she left, and he stood looking at the door, and it took him a long time to realize that she had understood, in a minute and with two fingers, something Corvus had taken months to explain to him.

---

The blood comes down over the three stains and opens like ink in water.

Josh watches it do what he asks. It spreads across the foot of the parchment, covers the signatures, absorbs them. It draws a slow spiral, each turn tighter than the last. And it evaporates. A dark smoke, very thin, that rises half a hand's breadth and dissolves in the still air of the back room.

The stains are gone.

His forearm burns. The veins have darkened from wrist to almost the elbow, visible beneath the skin like lines of ink, and they're slow to clear. Slower than last time. Always a little slower than last time.

"Your turn," he says.

Arabella takes her hands out of her pockets.

She doesn't ask what needs erasing. She already knows; she measured all of it while the supplier was opening the safe. She lays two fingers on the parchment, right where the signatures were. Then on the table. Then on the edge of the safe, on the stained mirror, on the floor where, a year ago, three drops fell that no one cleaned up properly.

The patches move.

Josh has seen them move many times and still isn't used to it. They climb her neck like something alive, cross her cheek, form and unform on the back of her hand at a rhythm that has nothing to do with her pulse. The longer the work lasts, the faster. At the fourth touch, her breathing shortens. At the fifth, she leans for a second on the edge of the table. She says nothing. She finishes.

When she straightens she is paler, and the patches are still moving, slowly, like water taking its time to settle after someone has thrown a stone.

"Clean," she says. "The parchment, the safe, the floor. If an Auror sticks his wand in here, he'll find a silver shop."

"It is a silver shop," says the supplier from the curtain.

"Now it is."

Josh folds the blank parchment, ties it with the same cord, and hands it back to the man.

"Keep it or burn it. It no longer matters." A brief pause. "And close for a few days. Say you're ill."

"And if they come?"

"If they come, you'll show them spoons."

The man nods many times in a row. Josh pulls down his sleeve. Buttons it. The burning is still there, beneath the cloth, dull, and it will stay a while.

---

Outside, in the alley, Arabella leans against the wall again. She looks at the back of her hand, where the patches haven't entirely settled. She waits.

"Three weeks ago Thursday," she says, without looking at him, "they picked up someone called Mercer in a back room two streets from here. Alone. At seven sharp."

"I heard something."

"All of Knockturn heard something. They say someone sold him out." She looks up. "Funny coincidence, isn't it? That his pact gets fulfilled the very same Thursday they pick him up."

"Pacts are fulfilled when they're fulfilled," says Josh. "The twelfth shipment arrived when it was supposed to. What happened to Mercer after he received it wasn't in any clause."

"No. I suppose it wasn't."

She looks at him a moment longer. The scar on her lip doesn't tighten. Josh holds her gaze just enough, and then lowers it to her hand, to the patches still moving.

"How much?" he asks.

"Forty."

He raises an eyebrow, barely.

"Last time it was twenty-five."

"Last time nobody had been arrested the Thursday before." She shrugs. "Jobs with a history charge for the history."

Josh counts forty Galleons from a bag inside his robes and gives them to her. She pockets them without counting. She already knows what forty weighs.

They stand a moment in silence. The alley is almost empty; a veiled woman passes on the other side without looking at them, dragging something that clinks.

"One more thing." Arabella points at his sleeve with her chin, without touching it. "That stuff you've got left."

He doesn't look at his arm. He doesn't need to.

"What about it?"

"It shows. Not on your skin. In how you move your hand." A pause. "I can take it off you. I've got two fingers free and my afternoon doesn't end till six. I'll charge you the same as the parchment."

"No, thank you."

"I know you won't." She says it without surprise, almost bored, like someone completing a formality she knows by heart. "You never say yes. I offer anyway, for the record."

"For whose record?"

"Somebody's." The scar tightens, barely. "Someone has to keep count of everything you won't get rid of, Greengrass. You clearly don't."

He doesn't answer. She doesn't expect him to. She looks down the alley, toward the exit onto Diagon Alley, and then back at him.

"And the parchment?" asks Josh. "Did you check the floor under the safe?"

Arabella looks at him. Not with suspicion: with something flatter, more tired.

"I already answered that inside."

It's a dry sentence. Nothing more. But he hears it land, and for half a second he can't find the moment she told him. He searches. He finds it: *the parchment, the safe, the floor.* It's there. She said it, in those exact words, less than five minutes ago.

"Right," he says. "Sorry."

She says nothing. She looks at him a second longer than courtesy requires, and this time Josh doesn't hold her gaze; he lowers it first. He realizes he lowered it first. He doesn't know whether she realized.

"You need to get back to your school," says Arabella, pushing off the wall. "The prefects won't wait for you."

"Five o'clock."

"I know. It's always five o'clock with you."

She leaves without saying goodbye, hands in her pockets, toward the mouth of the alley. Halfway there she looks at the back of her hand. The patches have almost stopped moving.

---

Hogsmeade receives him at a quarter past four with the same white light and the same smell of burnt sugar.

He appears behind the hedge, where no one looks. Adjusts his scarf. Walks back to the high street along the dirt path, turns at the post office, goes into Honeydukes. He buys Chocolate Frogs and a bag of Sugar Quills, and pays the shopkeeper with his right hand, because the left is still burning.

At five he's on the high street, among the seventh-years, listening to someone complain that the Three Broomsticks was too crowded. At half past five he passes through the castle gates with the last group.

No one asks where he's been. He has the Honeydukes bag in his hand and the bored air of someone who spent the afternoon eating Chocolate Frogs.

That night, with the curtains drawn, he unbuttons his left cuff.

The veins have cleared. Almost. A shadow remains, barely, in the crease of the wrist, where the skin is thinnest. It isn't ink. It isn't a bruise. It's what remains when something dissolves and never quite leaves.

He looks at it for a while. He thinks of two free fingers and an afternoon that didn't end until six.

He buttons his sleeve again.
