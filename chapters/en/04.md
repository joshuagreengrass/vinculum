# Chapter 4 — Macula

The Hogwarts kitchens are in the basement, behind a painting of a fruit bowl, and to reach them you have to go down the Hufflepuff corridor.

Josh has known that since first year. What he didn't know is that in mid-October, for an entire week, that corridor stops being a corridor.

He hears it before he sees it. Music from some enchanted gramophone, laughter, the dull thud of many feet on stone. Then the light: garlands of small pumpkins floating beneath the vaulted ceiling, paper lanterns changing color every few seconds, braziers burning in every corner, as if someone had decided the Scottish cold wasn't invited. The whole corridor is full of people in costume. A cardboard dragon with four human legs. Two ghosts who aren't ghosts. A fourth-year dressed as a mandrake complaining loudly that nobody understands his costume.

A handwritten sign, stuck to the wall with a Sticking Charm, reads *Costume Week — hosted by Hufflepuff — everyone welcome (yes, Slytherin too)*.

Josh reads it twice. The second time, it almost amuses him.

He isn't in costume. He's wearing his uniform robes, the green and grey scarf, his sleeves buttoned. He moves through the crowd with his hands in his pockets and his head down, hair over his eyes, with the clear intention of reaching the fruit-bowl painting, tickling the pear, asking some elf for something to eat, and getting back to the dungeon before anyone speaks to him.

Someone speaks to him.

"Greengrass?"

It isn't a question. It's an observation, said out loud in a tone of genuine surprise, the way one finds an object somewhere it doesn't belong.

Josh turns around.

It's Scamander. Amatis Scamander, from Hufflepuff, from Care of Magical Creatures on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Small, her hair in loose curls escaping in every direction, her brown skin scattered with freckles. He knows her by sight. Five years sharing a class in which they almost never exchanged more than two sentences in a row, and almost always about some animal that had just bitten someone.

Today she has ears.

Feline ears, rounded, fixed to a headband lost among the curls. Spots painted on her face, her neck, her bare arms: black spots with golden centers, uneven, some smudged by the heat. A long tail coming out behind her that moves, slightly, because someone put a charm on it. Fabric claws on her fingertips.

Josh looks at her. It takes him a second to place it.

"A leopard?"

"Cheetah," she says, as if it were obvious. "From the comics. She's a villain. From *Wonder Woman*." She stares at him. "You have no idea what I'm talking about."

"None."

"She's an archaeologist who turns into a cheetah woman because of an ancient curse." She makes a gesture with the claws. "She's really good. Well, she's bad. But she's good at being bad."

"That sounds like a lot of people I know."

She laughs. A short, surprised laugh that she doesn't cover with her hand. Josh wasn't expecting her to laugh.

He looks at the costume again, top to bottom. The spots, the ears, the tail that keeps moving on its own.

"I have to admit," he says, "that all of a sudden I like Care of Magical Creatures a lot more than I used to."

He says it in his usual voice: measured, dry, almost neutral. It's the tone of a joke that can be denied if it doesn't land. She hears it, opens her mouth, closes it. Color rises to her face, a pink that shows even under the painted spots.

"Oh," she says. "Okay. Wow." She adjusts an ear that wasn't crooked. "I didn't know Greengrass made jokes."

"I don't."

"You just made one."

"It was a comment about the subject."

"Uh-huh. About the subject." The tail moves faster. She doesn't seem to notice. "So what are you doing here, then? You're not in costume. Not even a little. Not even ears."

"I was going to the kitchens."

"The kitchens? You know how to get into the kitchens?"

"For seven years."

"I thought that was a Hufflepuff secret."

"It is. You tell it very loudly."

She laughs again. Someone bumps into her from behind by accident, a boy dressed as something with a lot of feathers, and she takes a step forward and ends up closer. The heat from the braziers is thick, sticky; half the corridor is in short sleeves or no sleeves at all, and Josh's shirt clings to his back under his robes.

She notices. She looks him up and down with the same attention she gives a new animal in class.

"Aren't you dying of heat?" she asks. "It's unbearable in here. And you in all that. In long sleeves."

And before he can answer, she reaches out, the hand with the fabric claws, and takes the cuff of his sleeve with two fingers. A light little tug, barely. Like someone checking the fabric. Like someone making sure something is on right.

---

He was ten and it was morning.

Tori was in the entrance hall in her travelling coat, the grey one, the one that had been too big for her since she'd lost weight. Outside, two cloaks were waiting on the gravel drive. He didn't know who they were. They didn't tell him. No one in that house had told him anything for a year.

He ran down the stairs. Barefoot again. She saw him and didn't scold him.

"You'll catch cold."

"Where are you going?"

"To do a job."

"What job?"

"One they asked me to do."

Her face was pale. More than usual. Her eyes shadowed, like on the bad nights, though the night before he hadn't heard anything behind the door. He'd heard her cough, nothing more. Softly. Like someone who doesn't want to be a bother.

She crouched in front of him. It cost her. He saw that it cost her and said nothing.

He was wearing the previous day's shirt, buttoned wrong, with one cuff loose. She saw it. She reached out and took the cuff with two fingers. A little tug. She straightened it. She fastened the button slowly, with fingers that trembled, as if it were important work. Then she smoothed the cloth over his wrist.

"There," she said. "Like that."

He didn't move.

"Tori."

"What?"

He didn't know what. He had nothing to say. He only wanted her not to stand up yet.

She pushed his hair off his forehead. It was long; they always cut it late.

"Don't wait up for me," she told him.

She stood up. It cost her again. She went to the door. One of the cloaks on the drive raised its head.

She didn't turn around.

He waited up for her. That night and the next. Sitting on the staircase, halfway down, with his shirt buttoned to the cuff.

---

"Josh?"

She's no longer holding his sleeve. She's taken her hand back. She's looking at him with her head tilted a little, the feline ears askew, and something in her face that isn't quite worry yet, but is on its way to being it.

Josh doesn't know how much time has passed. Two seconds. Three. The music goes on. The feathered boy is still behind her, now arguing with the mandrake. Nothing has changed in the corridor, except Scamander's face.

She asked him a question. About the heat. About the sleeves.

"I feel the cold," he says.

It comes out fine. The voice is the usual one. But she keeps looking at him, and he sees she didn't entirely believe him, that the answer came late and she registered the lateness, even if she doesn't know what to do with it.

"Okay," she says slowly. "You kind of… like…"

She makes a vague gesture with the claw, pointing at something in the air between them. She doesn't finish the sentence. She doesn't know how to finish it.

Josh does what he always does when there's a gap: he fills it with something else before anyone can look inside.

"Have you had dinner?"

She blinks.

"What?"

"Whether you've had dinner."

"It's…" She looks around, as if searching for a clock among the garlands. "Like nine. Dinner ended a thousand years ago. I ate… I think I ate a caramel pumpkin. Two. Why?"

"Because I haven't. And I was going to the kitchens. But the kitchens are past that corridor, and in that corridor there's a dragon with four legs."

"Those are the sixth-years. Don't look them in the eye."

"Then I'm not going to make it to the kitchens."

She narrows her eyes.

"Greengrass. Are you asking me to eat with you?"

"I'm informing you that I'm hungry, and that I know a place in Hogsmeade that's still open at this hour."

"Hogsmeade?" She says it too loudly. She drops her voice abruptly, and steps closer, and the painted spots shine with sweat under the light of the lanterns. "There's no Hogsmeade trip until next month. It's nine at night. It's a Thursday."

"I know."

"You could get expelled."

"Both of us."

She looks at him for a long while. This time he doesn't lower his eyes.

And then Amatis Scamander, of Hufflepuff, who in five years had never said more than two sentences in a row to him, smiles in a way he didn't know she could.

"No way am I changing out of the costume."

---

The passage begins behind a tapestry on the third floor and ends in the cellar of an abandoned house on the outskirts of the village. Josh has known it since fourth year. No one has ever asked him how he found it, because he's never told anyone.

Now he tells her, more or less. She asks questions the whole way.

"How many passages are there? Do you know? Is there a map? There has to be a map. Who made it? Was it always here or did someone dig it? Why is there water on the floor? Is that water? Give me your hand. No, the other one, this one has claws."

He gives her his hand. Hers is warm, and there's paint on her fingers.

They come out into the village through a rotten wooden door and onto a dirt street that smells of rain. Hogsmeade at night looks nothing like Hogsmeade on Saturdays: shop windows dark, signs still, a cat watching them from a roof and deciding they aren't worth the trouble. It's truly cold. She wraps her arms around her bare, painted arms.

Josh takes off his scarf and hands it to her without a word.

She looks at it. Green and grey. She wraps it around her neck anyway, over the spots and the ears.

"Now I look like a Slytherin villain," she says.

"She was always a villain."

"Yes, but now with style."

The Hog's Head is at the end of a side street, its window so dirty that the light inside comes out brown. No one asks anything at the Hog's Head. That's why it exists. The barman looks up when they come in, looks at Josh, looks at the girl with cat ears and a Slytherin scarf, and goes back to cleaning a glass that will never be clean.

They sit at a table at the back. Josh orders the only thing there is to eat at that hour: burgers. Two, with soda. The barman brings them without a word, wrapped in paper already going translucent with grease, and sets the bottles on the table with an offended look.

"This place is horrible," she says, delighted.

"It's discreet."

"It's horrible and discreet." She takes a bite of the burger. "And this is amazing. How is it so good?"

"Don't ask what's in it."

"Now I want to know what's in it."

They talk. Or rather, she talks, and he answers. She asks him why he chose Care of Magical Creatures if he never seems to enjoy it, and he tells her because it's the only class where no one expects him to speak. She tells him that her brother Theo once told her Slytherins don't eat, they just watch other people eat, and that now she'll have to write to him to set the record straight. She tells him about the costume, that she sewed it herself, that a friend charmed the tail and nobody knows how to turn it off. She asks him if he's ever read a comic. He tells her no. She tells him that then she's going to lend him one, and she won't take no for an answer.

She doesn't ask him about what happened in the corridor.

Josh waits for the question all through dinner. He has it ready, the answer. The heat, tiredness, a bad night. It doesn't come. She looks at him sometimes, out of the corner of her eye, between one question and the next, like someone checking again on something she left half understood. But she doesn't ask.

He doesn't know whether that reassures or alarms him.

---

They go back through the passage after midnight. At the tapestry entrance, on the dark third floor, she gives him back the scarf.

"Thanks for dinner."

"Thanks for not making me talk about the dragon."

"You owe me a comic. I mean, I owe you a comic. I'll lend you one. Same thing." She adjusts an ear. "Good night, Greengrass."

"Good night, Scamander."

She goes off down the corridor, the tail still moving on its own, and turns the corner without looking back.

Josh goes down to the dungeons. The dormitory is dark, the other beds closed. He sits on the edge of his own and begins to unbutton his sleeves, as he does every night, when no one is watching.

On the right cuff there's a stain.

It isn't blood. It's paint. Black, with a golden edge, smudged, the size of a fingertip: the print of two fingers that took his sleeve to check the fabric.

He looks at it for a long while.

He knows how to remove stains. He knows how to remove things much worse than a paint stain, from places much worse than a shirt cuff. A cleaning charm, two words, and the cloth is as it was before.

He folds the shirt with the stain on the inside and puts it away like that, at the bottom of his trunk.
