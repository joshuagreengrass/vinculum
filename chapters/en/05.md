# Chapter 5 — Ratio

On Monday, Josh realizes he's looking for her.

It isn't a decision. It's something he discovers afterward, the way one discovers a habit: he walks into the Great Hall for breakfast and, before sitting down, before looking at the Slytherin table, before anything else, his eyes have already crossed the room to the Hufflepuff table and found her. Curls. A cup held in both hands. A friend whispering in her ear and her laughing with her mouth full of toast.

Josh sits down. Serves himself. Butters his toast.

He registers it the way he would register any other piece of information: without comment. But he registers it.

---

On Tuesday there's Care of Magical Creatures.

They sit together. No one decides that either. He arrives at the gamekeeper's hut and she's already there, leaning on the fence, and when she sees him she shifts half a meter to the left without a word, like someone leaving free a place that was always someone else's. He takes it. During the lesson she talks to him quietly about the creatures, about the Latin names, about the time a pink Puffskein stole one of her earrings and she had to chase it all over her family's garden. He answers just enough. She doesn't seem to need more.

At the end of the lesson, while the others head back to the castle, she takes something out of her bag and puts it in his hands.

It's a thin magazine, glossy paper, its corners worn from passing through so many hands. On the cover, a woman in armor with a golden lasso fights another woman covered in spots.

"I told you I'd lend you one," she says. "It's the first one she's in. Take care of it. It's Theo's, and if a page gets folded he'll kill me."

"You or me?"

"Both of us. Theo is very democratic."

Josh looks at it. The pictures don't move. For someone raised in Greengrass Manor, that's the first thing that seems strange: still drawings, trapped in their panels, speech bubbles that never fade or change.

"They don't move," he says.

"No. That's the point." She shrugs. "You have to imagine what happens between one panel and the next. Your head fills it in."

Josh thinks of smooth spaces between one thing and the next. Of a board, a knife, and a cauldron.

"I'll read it," he says.

---

He reads it that night, with the curtains drawn. Twice.

On Wednesday he gives it back to her in the library, at a table at the back where she's writing a Herbology essay with her face almost pressed to the parchment. She looks up, sees the magazine, and her face lights up in a way he doesn't quite know how to receive.

"And?"

"The archaeologist makes a terrible decision on page eleven."

"Yes!" She says it too loudly. The librarian looks at them. She lowers her voice and leans across the table. "Yes. That's the thing. She knows it's terrible. She does it anyway."

"Because she thinks she has no other choice."

"But she does. She always does. That's why she's the villain."

Josh doesn't answer right away. She looks at him, waiting, quill still in hand.

"Sometimes the other choice is worse," he says. "It just doesn't show in the drawing."

She goes quiet for a moment. Then she smiles, slowly, like someone who has just found something in a place she didn't expect to look.

"Okay," she says. "I'll lend you the second one."

---

At some point that week, Josh starts to notice that she listens.

Not to him. To something else.

It happens rarely, and it never lasts. They're walking along the edge of the lake, or sitting on the stairs of the Astronomy Tower eating Chocolate Frogs she steals from somewhere, and suddenly she stops in the middle of a sentence. She tilts her head. Her eyes settle on a point in the air that is neither him nor anything. One second. Two. And then she comes back, and picks up the sentence exactly where she left it, as if nothing had happened.

He knows that gesture. He knows it from the inside.

He doesn't ask her. She didn't ask him either.

---

On Thursday, the Care of Magical Creatures lesson is at the edge of the Forbidden Forest.

The professor leads them to a clearing among the trees, where the afternoon light comes in sideways, green and cold. In the middle of the clearing there is a bucket of raw meat. Nothing else. The professor asks for silence, and they wait.

"Thestrals," he announces, quietly. "Some of you will see them. Most of you won't. Those who don't, don't feel bad: it's good news."

Josh sees them.

They come out from between the trees without a sound, one, then another, then three more. Skeletal, black, leathery wings folded against their sides and white eyes without pupils. They approach the meat with a calm that has nothing animal about it. The first to arrive lowers its head and tears off a piece, and half the class stifles a gasp, because they see the meat disappear into the air without anything touching it.

Josh doesn't gasp. He watches them eat.

Beside him, Atis doesn't gasp. She's still, her eyes fixed on the thestral that's eating, and it takes Josh a second to understand why: she's looking at them too, not at the meat.

"Can you see them?" she whispers.

He takes a while to answer.

"Yes."

She looks at him, not at the clearing. She doesn't ask whom he saw die. It shows on her face that she's about to and decides not to, and he watches her decide it, and doesn't know what to do with that.

"Me too," she says softly. "Since this summer."

Josh doesn't ask. It's the only thing he knows how to do well in that moment: not ask, like her.

She tells him anyway.

"My dad." Her eyes are on the thestrals, not on him. "In August. He left the house one morning, like always, and didn't come back. Nobody knows where he is. They searched for him. They say they're still searching." She shrugs, a small gesture that doesn't quite come off. "But in September I started seeing these. So…"

She doesn't finish the sentence. She doesn't need to.

The thestral that's eating raises its head for a moment. Its white eyes pass over the class, over the two of them, and return to the meat.

"He loved them," Atis goes on. "He used to say people fear them for what they mean, not for what they are. That they're some of the calmest animals he ever knew." A crooked smile escapes her. "The first time I saw them, I started crying in the middle of the garden. And not from fear. Because I understood what they meant."

Josh wants to say something. He searches for what. He has nothing that doesn't sound like a formula, a condolence for the record, the phrases spoken at the wakes of families like his, where no one cries in front of anyone.

He says nothing. She doesn't seem to expect him to.

One of the younger thestrals gets too close to the others. There's a sharp movement, a snap of teeth, and the young one backs off with a cut on its flank. A long cut, shallow. The blood runs down its leg.

It's dark.

Almost black against the black hide, thick, dense as oil. It doesn't drip right away: it stays a moment in the wound, gleaming, still, as if waiting for something. As if waiting for someone to ask it.

---

He was fourteen and it was the first time they let him watch without hiding.

It was night. A field, somewhere in the north, the grass flattened and burned in circles. It smelled of smoke and of something sweet, rotten. People stood around in a semicircle, in cloaks and hoods. He didn't know the faces. He'd been told the faces didn't matter.

There were bodies in the grass.

Five. Six. He didn't count them. People who had gone out on a mission two days earlier and hadn't come back, and no one had gone looking for them, and now they were there, thrown down in the field like things that fall out of someone's pocket. One had a boot on and the other off. He looked at the boot for a long time.

"Pawns," said someone beside him, the way one mentions the weather. "That's what they're good for, in the end. For this."

An older voice spoke from the center of the semicircle.

"The legacy wishes to see what the house of Greengrass can offer it."

He stepped forward. His knees were shaking and no one noticed because the cloak was long.

He pushed up his sleeve. He cut his forearm with the dagger, the black one, the one with the runes, and said the words, and felt his eyes go cold, as if ice water had gotten into them, and the world turned grey and white and very sharp.

The blood didn't fall.

It rose. It stretched from the cut in thin threads, one for each body, upward, toward the black sky, taut as ropes. He felt them pull. He felt them in his arm, in his chest, behind his eyes.

The bodies got up.

Clumsy. Slow. One first, then the others. The one with a single boot got up last, and stood with its head tilted, looking toward him with eyes that no longer looked at anything.

No one applauded. Someone, in the semicircle, let out a slow breath.

"The legacy recognizes its own," said the old voice.

He let go of the threads. The bodies fell. He didn't fall, though he wanted to.

Afterward they gave him a chair. Literally. In a room with a long table, that same night, somewhere with a fireplace, someone pulled out a chair for him and told him to sit. It was the first time he sat at that table.

His skin was like wax. The veins almost green, beneath. No one said anything about it.

Later, alone, he threw up in a bathroom with silver taps. Then he washed his face, pulled down his sleeve, buttoned it.

He went back to the table.

---

"Josh?"

The young thestral is gone. The professor is tending another's flank, or explaining something about diet, or both. The class murmurs. Atis has her fingers on his sleeve again, not tugging, just resting there.

"Are you okay? You went…"

She doesn't finish. He knows what she was going to say. Pale. Far away. Strange.

"I'm fine." The voice comes out even. "It's a smell. The meat."

She looks at the meat. Then she looks at him. They both know she also sees what's eating it.

She doesn't believe him. He knows because this time she doesn't hide it: she looks at him with a still attention, the kind she uses with creatures she doesn't understand yet. And then she takes her fingers off his sleeve, and takes his hand directly, right there, at the edge of the forest, with half the class around them.

She says nothing more. She squeezes his hand once. Lets go.

Josh keeps looking at his hand all the way back to the castle.

---

On Friday he walks her to the lake after dinner.

There's no reason. She says she wants to see whether the giant squid comes to the surface when it's cold, and he says no, it never comes up when it's cold, and she says then they'll find out, and they walk.

The water is black and still. Across it, the castle windows light up one by one. The air smells of wet leaves and of something burning far off, at the gamekeeper's hut.

"It's not coming up," he says.

"Wait."

They wait. The squid doesn't come up.

"I told you."

"You told me." She laughs and wraps her arms around herself. "Are you always right?"

"Almost always."

"That's very annoying."

"So I'm told."

She turns to look at him. Her nose is red with cold and her freckles barely show in the dusk. She looks at him for a long while, head tilted a little, the same gesture as when she listens to things no one else hears.

"What happened in class?" she asks. "With the thestrals. And in the corridor. Last week."

It isn't an accusation. It's a question, asked slowly, like someone leaving a door ajar.

Josh has the answer ready. He's had it all week. The heat, tiredness, the smell of the meat.

He doesn't use it.

"Someday I'll tell you," he says.

She thinks about it. Nods.

"Okay. Someday."

And then she stands on tiptoe, because she's small and he doesn't bend down, and kisses him.

It's brief. Clumsy at the edges. It tastes of chocolate and cold. He doesn't close his eyes at first, because he wasn't expecting it, and then he does. When she pulls back her cheeks are burning, a pink that shows even in that light.

"That was a comment about the lake," she says.

"About the lake?"

"About the squid. That it didn't come up."

Josh laughs.

He hears it before he understands it's him. A short, strange laugh, from he doesn't know where. She hears it too, and her eyes widen, and she smiles in a way he'd like to keep somewhere.

Then the coin burns in his pocket.

---

It's a Galleon. He always carries it in the inner pocket of his robes, on the left side, against his ribs. It looks like any other Galleon. It isn't.

Josh doesn't take it out in front of her. He brings his hand to his chest like someone adjusting his robes.

"I'm cold," he says. "We should go back."

"Now you're cold."

"I've always been cold. I feel the cold."

"Uh-huh."

They walk back. At the castle door she says a short "see you" and looks back twice as she heads toward the stairs down to Hufflepuff. The second time, he raises his hand.

When he loses sight of her, he goes into an empty classroom on the first floor and closes the door.

He takes out the Galleon. It's still warm. On the rim, where ordinary Galleons carry the serial number of the goblin who minted them, the numbers have moved. Rearranged themselves. Now they form letters.

*MERCER GAVE FOUR. SAT.*

Josh reads it once.

He reads it again.

Four.

He had said three. He said it at the Manor, in his father's armchair, in front of Corvus, with the certainty of someone reading a list he's already checked twice. *He knows three names. All three are below anything that matters.* He had calculated it. He had calculated it carefully.

He searches for the fourth name.

He doesn't find it.

He goes over what he knew about Mercer. The dockside cell, the supplier, the shipments, the three names. One by one. He knows them, all three, knows where they live, knows what they know. There is no fourth.

Or there is, and he doesn't know it.

Or he knew it, and it isn't there.

He stays in the dark classroom with the Galleon in his palm. He doesn't know which of the three is worse. He doesn't know, for the first time in a long time, which of the three is the truth. There's a list in his head and in that list there's a smooth space, the size of a name, where something should be or where nothing ever was, and he can't touch it to find out which.

On Saturday, Corvus is going to ask him. He's going to ask in the same voice he uses to ask about the tea.

Josh closes his hand around the coin.

Outside, in the corridor, someone runs past laughing. A girl. It isn't her. He raises his head anyway.

And then, there, in the empty classroom, with a mistake in his hand that he doesn't know is his, Josh does the other calculation. The one he didn't want to do.

A week ago, the list of what he couldn't afford to lose was short, and he knew it by heart. Now it has one more name.

That account doesn't balance either.
