# Chapter 0 — Limen

The Aurors wipe their boots before coming in.

Josh registers it the way everything is registered in this house: without comment. The younger of the two does it carefully, first one sole and then the other, on the iron scraper bearing the family crest. The older one waits to see the other do it and copies him with a curt motion, like someone following a protocol that appears on no form.

"Mr. Greengrass." The older one holds out a sealed parchment. "Ordinary inspection of property and artifacts, pursuant to postwar asset-control regulations. Your presence as heir is recorded as mandatory."

"And here I am." Josh receives the parchment with both hands, the way one receives something one intends to respect. "Come in, please. My apologies for the cold. The house isn't used to receiving visitors at this time of year."

At any time of year, really. But September is a half-truth, and half-truths are the ones that hold up best.

Outside, Wales is doing what it does in autumn: a drizzle that never quite makes up its mind, the green of the hills fading toward ochre, the oaks along the drive shedding their first leaves onto the gravel. Josh left Hogwarts at seven in the morning with a signed exit permit in the inner pocket of his robes, and he has to be back before dinner. The Ministry asked for the heir. The school handed over the heir. No one asked the heir whether he had Potions second period.

He's wearing his uniform, tie neatly knotted, the green and grey scarf around his neck. Long sleeves, buttoned at the wrist. Always.

"Elf."

The creature appears at his side without a sound, ears low.

"The lamps in the east wing. And tea in the drawing room for when the gentlemen are finished."

"That won't be necessary," says the older one.

"It's necessary for me," Josh replies, with a minimal smile. "My mother would never forgive me for receiving Ministry officials without offering them tea."

The Auror looks at him a second longer than courtesy requires. Josh holds his gaze just enough and then lowers it to the parchment, as if reading. The mention of his mother wasn't a slip. Nothing he says aloud in front of an Auror is. It's a useful reminder, delivered gently: *you already took the ones you had to take. All that's left here is a boy on a school permit.*

---

They go through the ground floor first.

The older one carries the list. He's a man of economical gestures, the kind who has spent twenty years taking inventories in houses like this one and no longer expects to find anything, but looks anyway, because looking is what he's paid for. He points his wand at each display cabinet, murmurs a detection charm, takes notes. The younger one follows a step behind. His hair is short, freshly cut, and he has a way of looking at things as if he were weighing them.

Josh knows him. He's been selling him information for a year and a half.

It doesn't show. Not even the young man carries it on him, and Josh appreciates that: some people, once they know a secret, start carrying it on their shoulders. Not this one. This one looks at the silver cabinets, the candelabra, the grandfather clock, with the same professional interest as his partner, and when he speaks to Josh he does so with the exact distance appropriate between an Auror and the son of two convicts.

"Does this clock work?" asks the older one.

"It's run four minutes slow for as long as I can remember," says Josh. "No one in the family ever wanted to fix it. I believe it was considered in poor taste."

The young one writes something down. The older one passes his wand in front of the clock face; the charm finds nothing, because there is nothing to find. It's a clock that runs slow.

They go up to the first floor by the main staircase. Josh leads, one hand on the banister, setting the pace without hurrying it. Behind him, the Aurors' footsteps sound different on the wood: the older one's, heavy and even; the younger one's, lighter, a little irregular, as if he were stopping to look.

The portraits watch them pass.

The gallery along the main corridor is long and poorly lit on purpose. Twelve generations of Greengrasses hang on the walls in dark frames, and almost all of them pretend to sleep whenever someone from the Ministry walks in. One pretends nothing. A man with a white ruff and a narrow gaze, painted three centuries ago, follows the Aurors with his eyes and says, to no one in particular:

"In the old days they at least had the decency to announce themselves by owl."

"An owl came," says Josh without stopping. "Two weeks ago. You were asleep."

The portrait huffs. The young Auror almost smiles. The older one does not.

Halfway down the gallery there is an empty space.

It isn't an obvious gap. No one who didn't know the house would notice: the wall has the same dark green paper as the rest, the same moldings, the same dust along the edges. But between the portrait of his grandmother and that of a great-uncle who died in Egypt there is more distance than between any other pair of frames. Precisely the measure of a medium-sized canvas. As if someone had measured the spot and then decided not to fill it.

Josh doesn't look at that space. Nor does he avoid it. He walks past it at the same pace he walks past everything else, and in his head, where no one can hear, he notes it with the coldness of an inventory: *there is nothing there. No one in this house ever commissioned that portrait, and no one in this house is going to ask why.*

The older Auror doesn't look at it either. The younger one does, for a second, and keeps walking.

---

The library takes them forty minutes.

The older one is methodical: shelf by shelf, spine by spine, the charm drawn in a slow sweep that makes the air barely hum. He finds what he expected to find in the library of a pure-blood family: histories of lineage, treatises on ancient herbology, a couple of volumes of defensive hexes catalogued and permitted for decades. He notes the titles that require registration. None requires confiscation.

Josh waits by the window, hands clasped behind his back. He doesn't look toward any particular point in the room. That's the hard part of inspections, harder than the courtesy: knowing exactly where what they're looking for is and not letting your eyes drift there, not once, not by reflex. The grimoires aren't in this room, nor in any room that appears on a floor plan. But the body knows where they are even when the mind decides not to think about it, and the body is precisely what an experienced Auror learns to read.

The older one has experience. Josh offers him a still back and a bored profile.

"Do you spend much time here, Mr. Greengrass?"

"I spend term at Hogwarts, sir. Here I read, in summer."

"What do you read?"

"What's on your list." Josh turns his head slightly. "Though I confess ancient herbology is more useful to me for my N.E.W.T.s than lineages."

The older one writes down something that probably has nothing to do with herbology.

---

They finish just after eleven. The second-floor bedrooms, the east wing with its newly lit lamps, the storeroom in the cellar, the stables empty since before Josh was born. Nothing. The older Auror spreads the report on the marble table in the entrance hall, signs it, and passes it to Josh to sign as heir.

Josh reads every line before signing. Not because he distrusts the contents, but because signing without reading is exactly what's expected of an eighteen-year-old eager to get back to school, and he doesn't do what's expected of him when there's an Auror watching.

He signs. *Joshua Greengrass.* The handwriting even, slanted, the same as always. He looks at it half a second longer than necessary, like someone checking precisely that.

"If during the year you recall any property that should be added to the report," says the young Auror as he rolls up the parchment, "you may write to the office. We'd appreciate it before the end of the month."

It's a formula. It appears at the foot of every report. The older one doesn't even look up.

"There's a set of silver goblets in the cellar storeroom," says Josh. "Seven, I think. I never knew whether there were eight. I'll confirm on Thursday and write to you."

"Thursday is fine."

That's all. Two inventory sentences, spoken in the same bored tone as the ones before, in front of a man who has spent twenty years listening to inventory sentences and no longer hears them.

The young one does hear them. *Thursday:* the day. *Seven:* the hour. *I never knew whether there were eight:* he'll go alone, without an escort. The rest—the name, the exact back room in Knockturn where Silas Mercer receives the crates that later go north—will arrive tonight by the usual route, on paper, at an address that appears on no report.

For a year and a half the young one has believed he's buying information from a boy who grew up too close to the rot. Close. Not inside. Josh has never corrected him.

The older one puts the report in his case. He takes his leave with a brief bow. The young one, with an identical one, calculated not to be any different. Josh sees them to the door.

The elf opens both oak doors before anyone asks. Outside, the drizzle continues. The older one goes out first. The young one pauses a moment on the threshold to turn up the collar of his cloak, and in that moment he is exactly there: one boot inside, one boot outside, his silhouette cut out against the grey light of the drive, the Auror's cloak darkening at the shoulders in the rain.

Josh watches him cross.

---

The door was open and cold was coming in.

It was the second time. The first he hadn't seen: he'd been asleep, or they'd made him sleep, and the next morning his father was gone and no one explained anything. Not this time. This time he heard the boots in the entrance hall and went down barefoot to the middle of the staircase before Astoria caught up with him.

She caught up with him. She didn't go any further down. She knelt on the step above, behind him, and put her hands over his eyes.

Her hands were hot. Too hot. They trembled, a fine, constant tremor he could feel on his eyelashes. She smelled of the ointment they put on her chest on the bad nights. She breathed close to his ear, short, labored, as if climbing those steps had cost her more than climbing a few steps costs.

"Shh."

Nothing more. She didn't tell him not to look. She covered his eyes, and that was saying it.

He looked anyway.

Between Astoria's fingers there were slits, and in the slits, pieces. A dark cloak. Another. Someone's hand at his mother's elbow, not gripping, only guiding, the way you guide someone who already knows the way. His mother's hair pinned up, perfect, at that hour. The door. The grey outside. The cold climbing the staircase to his bare feet.

His mother stopped on the threshold.

No one made her; she stopped. She turned her head, not her body, toward the staircase. Toward them. Astoria's fingers closed a little more, the slit grew narrower, and he tilted his head so as not to lose it.

His mother looked at them. One last time.

And nodded.

Just once. Short. The way you nod at something understood without needing to be said.

Then she crossed.

Astoria's hands didn't move until long after the door had closed. He didn't take them away. They stayed like that, on the staircase, she trembling and he still, and the cold took a long time to leave the entrance hall, and he didn't know what that gesture had meant, and later he thought he did, and later he was no longer sure of

---

"Well? How was the inspection?"

The door is closed.

Josh didn't hear it close. Nor did he hear the fireplace in the entrance hall, but there is green ash on the hem of the cloak of the man in front of him, two paces away, in the exact spot where a moment ago there was drizzle and an Auror turning up his collar. Corvus Lestrange removes his gloves finger by finger and looks at him with the patience of someone who has already asked the question and has no intention of asking it twice.

Josh calculates. The door, the elf, the fireplace, the gloves. Three seconds. Four, at most.

Nothing.

"Ordinary," he says. "As the owl indicated."

It sounds exactly as it should. Corvus nods, satisfied with that or with something else, and hands his gloves to the elf without looking at it.

"Let's go through, then. I understand there's tea."

There is tea. The elf served it in the drawing room for two Ministry officials who didn't want it, and now a Lestrange is drinking it, seated in the armchair that belonged to Josh's father, legs crossed, holding the cup with two fingers like a collector's piece. Josh sits across from him. He takes nothing.

"Two," says Corvus. "Was one of them yours?"

"The young one."

"And the other?"

"Twenty years of inventories. He found nothing because there's nothing to find where he looks." Josh folds his hands over his knee. "He registered three titles from the library and a clock that runs slow. Tomorrow the report will be in a Level Two file, and in a month no one will remember why it was done."

"That is what the Ministry does with the houses of those who paid," says Corvus. "It visits them. It counts them. It convinces itself that counting is the same as seeing." He takes a sip. "The legacy has always been grateful to the Ministry for its faith in inventories."

Josh doesn't smile. Corvus didn't expect him to.

"What did you sell them this time?"

The question arrives with no change in tone, in the same voice in which he asked about the tea. That's how these questions are asked in the high command: as if they were minor. As if everyone in this room didn't know exactly what Josh does on Thursdays.

"Mercer."

Corvus sets the cup on the saucer.

"Silas Mercer?"

"He moves the crates from the back room up north. On Thursday he receives a shipment at seven. He'll be alone."

"Mercer has been with us for six years."

"Mercer has been with a dockside cell that doesn't know your name. Or mine." Josh speaks slowly, unhurried, like someone reading a list he's already checked twice. "He knows three names. All three are below anything that matters. When they arrest him, he'll talk, and he'll give them those three, and the Ministry will take a year to connect them to each other. When it does, they won't lead anywhere."

"An isolated incident."

"They like isolated incidents. They can be filed."

Corvus considers it. Not for long; there isn't much to consider. Josh has already done the math for him, and Corvus knows it, and they both know the other knows it.

"A man without a name," he says at last. "The legacy does not mourn those who have no name worth mourning."

Josh thinks, without having decided to, of the gallery upstairs. Of the distance between two frames.

He sets it aside.

"That's why I chose him."

"Of course." Corvus picks up the cup again. "And in exchange, your short-haired young man takes back to the office the feeling that he's one step away from something. And he comes back. And he keeps believing you."

"He keeps buying from me."

"Which is not the same."

"No. But it's cheaper to maintain."

For the first time all morning, something in Corvus's face resembles approval. It doesn't last.

"You realize," he says, and his voice drops a tone, not harder, only more precise, "that every name you hand them is a name you owe us."

"Every name I hand them is a name I don't hand them," Josh replies. "I choose which."

"As long as the accounts balance, Greengrass."

"The accounts balance."

Corvus looks at him for a long moment over the rim of the cup. It's the same look the older Auror gave him in the entrance hall, a second longer than courtesy, and Josh holds it exactly the same way: just enough, and then he lowers it.

"For now," says Corvus.

He sets down the cup, rises, smooths his cloak. At the drawing-room doorway he stops, without turning around.

"Your father kept that armchair facing the window. He liked to see who was coming up the drive."

"I know."

"You ought to put it back where it was."

Josh doesn't answer. Corvus doesn't wait for an answer. The fire in the entrance hall flares green for an instant and then is just fire again.

---

The Portkey the Ministry authorized for the return trip leaves at five, from the gate.

Josh adjusts his scarf. He checks the buttons on his sleeves, one and then the other. He tells the elf to put out the lamps in the east wing and not to leave the tea out, that no one else is going to drink it. The elf vanishes without a sound.

The entrance hall falls silent. Upstairs, in the gallery, the portraits go on pretending to sleep even though there's no longer anyone from the Ministry to pretend for. The staircase is empty. The steps, worn in the middle by twelve generations of feet, keep no mark of two children halfway down the stairs.

Josh opens the door himself.

Outside, the drizzle is exactly the same as it was at seven in the morning. He pauses on the threshold for as long as it takes to turn up the collar of his robes. One boot inside, one boot outside.

Then he crosses.

Only on the gravel drive, with the fine rain on his face and the gate already in sight, does he realize that before leaving he looked toward the staircase.

And that he nodded.
