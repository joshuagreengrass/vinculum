# Chapter 1 — Signum

Thursday begins like any other Thursday, and that is the first thing Josh makes himself register.

He wakes before the others. Not from anxiety—he tells himself so, in that word, and discards it—but because for years now he has woken before the others. The seventh-year dormitory lies in green half-light. The lake's light comes through the high windows with its usual slowness, filtered through fathoms of water, and something large passes on the other side of the glass without hurry and is lost in the dark. The other beds have their curtains drawn. The breathing behind them is not his.

He dresses in silence. Shirt, tie, robes. The buttons on his sleeves, one and then the other, fastened at the wrist until the cloth shows nothing. Before leaving he looks at his hands. They are still. They are his.

At seven this evening, in a back room in Knockturn, Silas Mercer is going to receive a shipment alone.

Josh goes down to breakfast.

---

The Great Hall smells of toast and rain. The enchanted ceiling repeats the sky outside: an even grey, low, an autumn that reaches Scotland before anywhere else. Josh sits at the end of the Slytherin table where he always sits, neither in the middle nor alone, at the exact distance that lets him hear everything without having to take part in anything.

Around him they talk about the usual. N.E.W.T.s, still far off and already heavy. A Quidditch match someone swears the referee handed over. A letter from a mother demanding, again, that someone seriously consider the Ministry as a career. Josh butters a piece of toast with the attention of someone doing something important.

At eight the post arrives. The owls spiral down beneath the grey ceiling, drop parcels and newspapers onto the tables, carry off crumbs. None comes down toward him. He didn't expect any to. Someone across the table opens *The Daily Prophet* and reads aloud a headline about the reform of broomstick regulations, and two people laugh, and the morning goes on.

"How was your day off, Greengrass?" asks a sixth-year, without malice, with the light curiosity of someone asking for the sake of it.

"A Ministry inspection at my family's house," says Josh. "They counted spoons."

"And were they all there?"

"One was missing. They found it in the Auror's pocket."

They laugh. He smiles just enough so the laughter doesn't feel alone. It's easy. A joke about the Ministry at the Slytherin table is common currency, accepted everywhere, compromising no one. And it lets him say the word *inspection* out loud, in front of everyone, in the most boring way possible, so that no one thinks of it again.

---

Potions is second period, in the dungeons.

The classroom is hot before the first fire is lit. Thirty cauldrons in rows, the vapor already beginning to rise from the back, the smell of cut herbs and metal. The professor writes the instructions for the Draught of Living Death on the blackboard—N.E.W.T. revision, he says, as if anyone in the room didn't already know it by heart—and within ten minutes half the class has rolled up their sleeves.

Josh doesn't roll up his sleeves. Ever. The heat glues his shirt to his back and dampens the nape of his neck beneath his hair, and he works anyway, cuffs buttoned and robes in place, as if the temperature were an opinion he doesn't share. No one comments. For seven years no one has commented.

He cuts the valerian root into even slices. Measures. Lights the fire beneath the cauldron with a short flick of his wand. The hawthorn settles into his hand as always, without protest. Beside him, a Ravenclaw boy is fighting a sopophorous bean that keeps slipping out from under his knife, hard and slick as a river stone.

"Crush it," Josh tells him without looking up. "With the flat side. It releases more juice."

The boy looks at him warily, the way one looks at advice that comes from the wrong table. Then he tries it. It works. He doesn't thank him, and Josh doesn't expect him to.

The next bean is smaller. The boy goes back to the edge, out of habit or pride, and the knife slips.

It isn't a serious cut. A clean slice on the pad of his index finger, the kind that stings more than it bleeds. But it bleeds. A fat, bright drop that forms at the fingertip and falls onto the wooden board, next to the valerian slices. Then another.

Red.

Truly red. Clear, almost cheerful, the color blood has when it is only blood. It spreads across the wood and the wood drinks it, and the edge darkens slightly, and Josh looks at it, and

---

The first time his blood obeyed him he was eleven and it was July.

The house was asleep. The elves were asleep, or doing whatever elves did at night, far away. For more than a year no one had gone up to the gallery at night, no one had opened the door at the end of the corridor, no one in that house had said certain names. He knew where the grimoires were. He had always known. It was only that before there had been someone who would have stopped him, and now there wasn't.

He chose the smallest ritual he could find. The book said it was the most docile. Erasing an old mark: a contract of an ancestor, sealed in blood almost two centuries before, fulfilled almost two centuries before. A rust-colored stain at the foot of a parchment. Nothing anyone would miss.

He used the silver letter opener from his father's desk. He held it for a long time before using it.

It hurt less than he'd expected. It came out red, at first. Red like anyone's. And then, outside, on his open palm, it darkened. It grew dense. Almost black. Almost oil.

It didn't fall.

He said the words from the book, quietly, pronouncing them badly, and the blood rose from his palm as if someone had called it by its name. It stretched. It came down over the old stain and opened across it like ink in water. It drew a slow spiral. And it was gone. A dark smoke, very thin, barely visible against the candlelight.

The stain was gone.

His forearm burned. He pushed up the sleeve of his pajamas and saw the veins, just slightly darker than before, like ink running beneath paper. He stayed looking at them until they cleared.

Then he looked at his hand.

He didn't recognize it. A second. Less. A pale hand with a cut on the palm, resting on a clean parchment, the hand of someone who had just done that. Then it was his again.

He bandaged it with a handkerchief. Cleaned the letter opener. Put the book back where it had been.

In the morning no one asked about the bandage. There was no one left in that house who knew what to ask.

---

The professor is already beside the Ravenclaw boy. A tap of the wand on the finger, a murmur, and the cut closes. Another tap on the board, and the blood vanishes from the wood as if it had never been there. The boy laughs, embarrassed. Someone calls him clumsy. The class goes on.

Josh has the knife in his hand. The valerian slices are on the board, even, the ones he cut. The cauldron simmers low.

He doesn't know whether he's already added them.

It isn't a large doubt. It's a small gap, the size of a gesture: the hand carrying the slices to the cauldron, or not carrying them. He can't find the memory of having done it. Nor of not having done it. There is a board, a knife, a cauldron, and between the three things a smooth space where something should be.

He doesn't stop. Stopping shows.

He looks at the cauldron. Without the valerian, the draught should still be the color of blackcurrant, thick, opaque. With the valerian, it should have turned a dark violet, cleaner, beginning to clear at the edges.

It is dark violet. It is clearing at the edges.

So he added them. The ones on the board are the leftovers. He cut too many. That makes sense. He always cuts too many, just in case.

He stirs seven times counterclockwise. Adds the juice of the bean. The draught clears, obedient, until it is as transparent as water. The professor passes behind his cauldron, leans in, nods.

"Impeccable, Mr. Greengrass."

"Thank you, Professor."

No one notices. He makes sure no one notices. And for the rest of the lesson, every time he looks at the leftover slices on the board, he goes over it again, the count, the color, the conclusion, like someone checking a door he already knows is locked.

---

Defense Against the Dark Arts is at three.

The lesson is on Inferi. Josh almost smiles when he reads it on the blackboard, and doesn't.

The professor is good at what she does. She explains clearly, without drama, with the confidence of someone who learned this after a war and not from a book. Inferi are not people, she says. There is no one inside. They are obedient bodies, sustained by the will of whoever raises them, and that will has a cost no wizard can pay for long. She draws the circle of fire on the blackboard, the only safe defense. She speaks of the war without naming it. Everyone knows what she's speaking of.

"And can it be traced?" asks someone from Hufflepuff. "Whoever raises them."

"Always," says the professor. "Dark magic leaves a trace. It always leaves a trace. The hard part is never finding it; it's getting there in time."

Josh writes the phrase in the margin of his parchment, in his even, slanted hand. *It always leaves a trace.* He looks at it a moment. Then he goes on taking notes on what she says about fire.

Outside, the tower clock strikes four. Then half past four. The light from the window drops from grey to a darker grey. The lesson ends, and the professor sets homework, and someone complains, and Josh puts away his quill, his parchment, his inkwell, in the same order as always.

Two hours and twenty minutes left.

---

He spends them in the library, because that's what any seventh-year with an essay due Monday would do. He writes the essay. It's good. He rereads it once and corrects a comma.

He doesn't look at the clock. He counts it another way: by the light in the high windows, by the number of students who get up and leave, by the moment the librarian starts putting out lamps at the back. When the next room empties, he knows what time it is without having looked at anything.

He goes down to dinner at ten to seven.

The Great Hall is full. The enchanted ceiling is already night: dense clouds, rain that never quite reaches the candles. Josh sits in his place. Serves himself. Cuts the meat into even pieces.

The tower clock begins to strike seven.

Josh doesn't stop. He brings the fork to his mouth on the third stroke. Chews on the fourth. Beside him, someone is arguing with someone about whether the Defense professor was or wasn't at the final battle; on the fifth stroke they raise their voices, on the sixth they laugh. On the seventh, Josh rests his fork on the plate and takes a sip of water.

And that's all.

Nothing changes in the Great Hall. The candles go on floating at the same height. The conversation goes on. In London, hundreds of miles away, in a back room that smells of wet wood and crates no one opens, a man without a name is receiving a shipment. Or has just received it. Or is being taken by the elbow, not gripped, only guided, the way you guide someone who already knows the way.

Josh can't know. He won't know tonight by any route other than the agreed one. He can't ask, can't write, can't look toward any window with more attention than usual.

Dinner ends. He takes dessert, because not taking dessert would be the first thing he's done differently all day.

---

The dormitory is once again in green half-light. Outside, on the other side of the glass, the lake is black.

The others fall asleep one by one. Josh reads in bed with the curtains drawn and a low light at the tip of his wand. He hasn't turned a page in twenty minutes. It doesn't matter. No one sees him.

After midnight, something moves at the foot of the bed.

It's a castle elf. Old, droop-eared, a tea towel bearing the Hogwarts crest tied on like a tunic. It holds an envelope in both hands, at the height of its face, the way one holds something that doesn't belong to one.

"For Mr. Greengrass," it whispers. "It came with the Ministry owl. Late. It was left in the Owlery."

Josh takes the envelope.

"Thank you, elf."

The elf vanishes without a sound.

The envelope is official parchment, sealed with purple wax and the Ministry crest. Inside is a single sheet with the printed letterhead of the Auror Office, Asset Control Section. Reference: inspection report, Greengrass Manor. Two lines, in a clerk's hand, unsigned:

*We confirm receipt of your information. There were seven.*

Josh reads it once. Then again.

To anyone else it's bureaucracy. An office filing an inventory of silver goblets and thanking the heir for his precision. To him it's a man, alone, without an escort, at the exact hour, in the exact place. A man who is now in a Ministry cell with three names in his mouth that lead nowhere. An isolated incident.

Seven. Not eight. No one else fell.

It went well.

He brings the sheet to the light of his wand and murmurs a short charm. The edge catches, curls, turns black. He lets it burn on the palm of his hand, without letting go, until nothing remains that could be read. The ash is light, almost nothing. He gathers it and lets it fall into the glass of water on the nightstand.

Then, with the curtains still drawn, he unbuttons his sleeves.

He does it rarely. Only like this, at night, alone. The scars are where they always are: those at the wrist, those on the forearm, the most recent ones, still pink, the ones he doesn't explain. He runs his eyes over them the way the older Auror ran his over the display cabinets. Without expecting to find anything. Looking anyway.

The oldest is on the palm of his left hand. A white line, very fine, almost erased. Eleven years old. July. A silver letter opener.

It is the oldest. It is the finest.

And it is the only one that, if he looks at it long enough, he still recognizes as his.
